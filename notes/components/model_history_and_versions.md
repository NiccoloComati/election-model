### 2016-06-29 · [A User’s Guide To FiveThirtyEight’s 2016 General Election Forecast](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/a-users-guide-to-fivethirtyeights-2016-general-election-forecast/)
**model change** · FiveThirtyEight 2016 presidential · explicit

The 2016 FiveThirtyEight presidential model was mostly the same as the 2008 and 2012 models, with no candidate-specific variables for Trump or Clinton, new procedures for third-party candidates such as Gary Johnson, and three published versions: polls-plus (polls plus economic index), polls-only, and a now-cast, which is polls-only told the election is today.

> There’s no special variable for Republican Donald Trump or Democrat Hillary Clinton.

### 2018-09-12 · [How FiveThirtyEight’s House, Senate And Governor Models Work](https://fivethirtyeight.com/methodology/how-fivethirtyeights-house-and-senate-models-work/)
**model change** · FiveThirtyEight 2020 Senate/House · explicit

2020 congressional changes: fundamentals formulas adjusted so higher partisanship makes partisan lean more predictive and candidate quality less; house effects computed mostly from polls in the same state or district; Election Day error raised 20 percent for COVID and mail voting; CANTOR similarity including expected mail-vote share; Cost of Voting Index; Deluxe became the interactive default.

*Numbers:* Election Day error increase = 20 percent (2020)

> higher partisanship is associated with the partisan lean of a state or district being more predictive of election outcomes, while it makes candidate quality factors (such as the candidate’s level of experience) less predictive.

### 2020-08-12 · [How FiveThirtyEight’s 2020 Presidential Forecast Works — And What’s Different Because Of COVID-19](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/how-fivethirtyeights-2020-presidential-forecast-works-and-whats-different-because-of-covid-19/)
**model change** · FiveThirtyEight 2020 presidential · explicit

The 2020 FiveThirtyEight presidential model differs modestly from 2016: more uncertainty components (economic uncertainty, news volume); an economic index extended back to 1880, adjusted for partisanship and with an economic forecasting component; extra Election Day error for pandemic turnout; COVID severity and mail-vote share as covariance factors; half the usual convention bounce adjustment; revised polling averages; a Cost of Voting Index adjustment; event handling for debates; and a single version with no polls-only, polls-plus or now-cast.

*Why:* COVID-19 rather than 2016 justified rethinking.

> We’re running only one version of the presidential model this year.

### 2024-06-26 · [2024 presidential election model methodology update](https://www.natesilver.net/p/model-methodology-2024)
**design principle** · general principle · explicit

Silver is conservative about changing election models between cycles. He holds that model-builders err too much toward 'fighting the last war', meaning they tinker with reasonable assumptions that happened not to work out or that made people angry, although leaning too far in either direction is possible.

*Why:* The goal is to fix what clearly needs fixing without fixing what isn't broken.

> In general, model-builders err too much toward “fighting the last war” — i.e. tinkering with things that didn’t work out great in the last election or made people mad at them

### 2026-11-03 · [How to judge an election model](https://www.natesilver.net/p/how-to-judge-an-election-model)
**limitation or failure mode** · FLIPR 2026 · explicit

Silver's pre-registered list of possible FLIPR changes: (1) treat polling errors as persistent for specific candidates such as Collins, against which he has a strong prior; (2) a fancier undecided-voter adjustment by state lean; (3) credit WAR or past margins for non-incumbents; (4) include non-individual fundraising sources; (5) account for candidate ideology; (6) improve partisan lean for midterm versus presidential electorates, registration and migration; (7) remove the expert-ratings layer; (8) force the House popular vote estimate closer to the generic ballot, currently 1 to 1.5 points apart by design; (9) use early voting, which would be a beast; (10) de-emphasize polling generally. Changes 1, 2, 4 and 10 would likely help Republicans in 2026 and 3, 5, 8 and 9 Democrats.

*Numbers:* House popular vote vs generic ballot gap = 1-1.5 points (intentional, 2026)

*Why:* Keeping a list ahead of time avoids being overly results-driven and fighting the last war.

> I always think it’s helpful to keep a mental list of these ahead of time so you’re not too overly driven by results and wind up fighting the last war.
