Senate fundamentals differ from House ones: the previous margin is less reliable because six years have passed; incumbency advantage is larger in smaller, more demographically distinct states; the model accounts for changes in state partisanship since the seat was last contested; and it uses a more advanced candidate-experience measure.

### 2018-09-12 · [How FiveThirtyEight’s House, Senate And Governor Models Work](https://fivethirtyeight.com/methodology/how-fivethirtyeights-house-and-senate-models-work/)
**method** · FiveThirtyEight 2018 midterm · explicit

Open-seat races use the non-incumbency factors (generic ballot, fundraising, partisan lean, scandals, experience, top-two primary results where applicable) plus the previous congressional result, which is much less reliable than with an incumbent. Previous results are not used in open governor races. In 2018, Pennsylvania redistricting was handled by population overlap, and California and Washington top-two primary results were used.

### 2023-12-07 · [The incumbency advantage is disappearing. Maybe it's the algorithms' fault.](https://www.natesilver.net/p/the-incumbency-advantage-is-disappearing)
**empirical regularity** · general principle · explicit

The high raw reelection rate for Congressional incumbents overstates the incumbency advantage. Fewer seats are competitive, and incumbents who expect to lose often retire, a selection effect. A regression controlling for these factors shows the Congressional incumbency advantage has declined substantially.

*Why:* Raw reelection rates confound incumbency with district competitiveness and strategic retirement.

> if you look carefully — such as by running a regression analysis to control for these different factors — you’ll find that the Congressional incumbency advantage has declined quite a bit too

### 2024-06-26 · [2024 presidential election model methodology update](https://www.natesilver.net/p/model-methodology-2024)
**model change** · 2024 presidential · explicit

The 2024 incumbency formula makes four changes. It adds an incumbency tier for presidential rematches, which assumes results are more persistent when the exact same two candidates run. It adds a variable for the incumbent party's popular-vote margin in the previous election. It adds a constant term. It weights recent elections more heavily in a regression fit on elections since 1880.

*Numbers:* regression sample start = 1880 year (incumbency regression)

*Why:* More robust going forward.

> the model now includes a third variable indicating how much the incumbent party won (or lost) the popular vote by in the previous election, and a constant term

### 2026-08-11 · [How FLIPR forecasts the midterm elections](https://www.natesilver.net/p/flipr-midterms-model-methodology)
**method** · FLIPR 2026 · explicit

Fancy fundamentals fit separate regressions for incumbent and open-seat races; races with appointed incumbents count as open seats. Incumbency handling uses congressional approval polls to gauge the climate for incumbents, a larger incumbency advantage in small or idiosyncratic states and districts with low CANTOR similarity, the incumbent's margin in their last competitive race adjusted for that year's environment and redistricting, whether the incumbent is in a first elected term or beat an incumbent last time (both treated as strengths), and a smaller advantage when new and old districts overlap little.

*Why:* First-termers lacked incumbency last time, so their last margin understates strength.

> we technically fit separate regressions for incumbent and open-seat races.
