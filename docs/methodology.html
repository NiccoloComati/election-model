Methods note

Replicating Nate Silver's presidential forecast

Rebuilt from his articles and published outputs, then checked against his day-by-day forecasts for 2020 and 2024.

How it works

Polls, a statistical estimate of each state, and an economic prior feed 40,000 simulated elections in 50 states, DC, and the Maine and Nebraska districts.

Weight polls

  • Weight grows with sample size, with diminishing returns
  • Better-rated pollsters count more; firms that poll a race often are capped
  • Influence fades over about six weeks, more slowly where polls are scarce

Adjust polls

  • Remove each firm's lean versus other polls of the same race, shrunk toward zero
  • Shift registered-voter polls to a likely-voter basis
  • Discount post-convention polls by about 2 points, fading over three weeks

Estimate states

  • Regression: national margin plus the state's lean from the last two elections
  • Demographic and regional terms catch states drifting from that lean
  • Regression weight falls with polling: about 25% in a lightly polled state, under 2% in Pennsylvania

Add fundamentals

  • Six economic indicators as z-scores, using data as first published
  • Regression on elections since 1880: economy, incumbency, last result, damped by polarization
  • Weight about 25% at 100 days out, zero on Election Day

Simulate

  • 40,000 elections with t-distributed (10 df) errors for fat tails
  • Demographic and regional swings make similar states miss together
  • Win probability is the share of simulations reaching 270 electoral votes

Anatomy of a simulated error

In each simulated election, a state's result is its forecast plus a random error. That error is the sum of four pieces, so states miss together the way they do in real elections.

nationalOne shift applied to every state, say 2 points toward Trump. It covers both how much the race can still move and a nationwide polling miss.
+
demographicA random swing for each voter group, say Hispanic voters 4 points right. States shift in proportion to their share of that group.
+
regionalA smaller swing shared by all states in a region, such as the Midwest.
+
stateNoise for each state alone. It is bigger where there are few polls.

The national piece shrinks as Election Day nears, in proportion to the cube root of the days left. It is also bigger when an uncertainty index is high: more undecided voters, more volatile polls, less polarization. Every piece is drawn from a t-distribution with 10 degrees of freedom, so big misses are likelier than under a normal distribution.

Where the parameters come from

Silver's writingAbout 100 articles give the structure and stated values.
His published outputsUnknowns are fitted to reproduce his poll weights, house effects and forecast spread.
HistoryElections since 1880 for fundamentals, since 1968 for poll movement and error.
Real-time dataEconomic series as first published on each date, not as later revised.
